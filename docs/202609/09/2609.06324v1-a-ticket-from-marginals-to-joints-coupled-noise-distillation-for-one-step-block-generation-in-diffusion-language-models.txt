Abstract 

Autoregressive language models commit one token per forward pass; diffusion language models commit a block of tokens over several steps. We ask whether a block can be committed in a single forward pass. We study this with a noise-conditioned masked denoiser: a data-independent Gaussian noise field is added to the mask embeddings so that, in principle, each sampled field selects one joint mode of the block. The established way of training such a model is to sample several fields per example and let them compete for the data, by winner-take-all or importance weighting. This gives the noise only coarse control: in our experiments, the information it carries grows roughly with the logarithm of the number of competing fields, and one-step outputs remain rarely coherent across the model sizes tested. We propose C ONDOR (Coupled-Noise Distillation for One-Step Readout). A noise-conditioned teacher is trained with a random number of masked positions and winner-take-all. A student proposes a one-step block, retains selected tokens, and learns from the block obtained when the teacher refills the other positions in several steps under the same noise field; a noise-free masked-LM term on the ground truth anchors the student. Human evaluation on TinyStories shows a large gain in one-step legality while different noise fields still yield different blocks, at one forward pass per block. 

# 1 Introduction 

Autoregressive (AR) language models generate text one token per forward pass (44, 54). Each token conditions on all previous ones, which makes the output coherent, but the number of sequential passes equals the number of tokens, and the cost of a long response is dominated by this sequential depth. Diffusion language models generate a block of tokens at a time and spend several steps on it (Figure 1). Continuous-latent models such as Diffusion-LM, ELF, and Cola (16, 21, 32) start a block from Gaussian noise and transport it to a latent representation through n denoising steps, decoding the latent to text at the end. Masked diffusion language models (dLLMs) such as LLaDA and LLaDA 2.1 (3, 40) start a block from mask tokens; at each step the model reads, for every masked position, a distribution over the vocabulary given the visible tokens, and commits several positions in parallel. Because these positions are predicted independently from the same context, the committed tokens can be mutually inconsistent (15, 25), and later steps edit or remask them (3, 22, 55, 60); n steps of commitment and correction take the block from fully masked to filled. In all of these models a block still takes several passes. Since large models have ample parameters, one may ask whether a whole block can be produced in a single forward pass. For a masked model the obstacle is the following. The mask token is the same at every masked position, so the cross-entropy-optimal prediction at each position is its marginal distribution given the visible tokens (41), and committing all positions at once samples from a product of marginals. Nothing in the input indicates which joint mode of the block is being 1arXiv:2609.06324v1 [cs.CL] 6 Sep 2026 generated. AR models carry this information in the prefix, latent-variable models in a per-sample latent z (28), and continuous diffusion models in the initial noise. A masked model has no counterpart within a step. We therefore give the model such a variable. A data-independent Gaussian noise field ε is added to the mask embeddings, and the model is asked to emit the whole block in one pass as a function of the prompt and ε: one sampled field ε (one ticket ) should yield one coherent block, and different fields different blocks (Figure 1d). The difficulty is to make the noise represent a mode. One approach constructs the coupling between the noise and the text from the text itself: a VAE-style encoder infers the latent from the target (VADD (57)), a continuous-latent diffusion model makes the noisy state a corrupted copy of the text (ELF, Cola), and PTP derives the auxiliary variable from an autoregressive sampler (9). Without such an encoder the coupling has to be learned from the data alone, and two objectives have been used for this. An importance-weighted multi-sample bound (IWAE (5)) samples k noise fields for the same training example and weights each by how well its output fits the data; the bound is dominated by the best-fitting field, so it is nearly satisfied as soon as one of the k fields explains the data, and the remaining fields are free to cover other modes. Winner-take-all (WTA) competition (18, 30) is the hard version of this: it also samples k fields but trains only the one whose output is closest to the data. Social GAN used it to give a noise-conditioned trajectory generator several modes (17), and LoRA-MCL applied it to language models (35). Both make the output depend on the noise, and we use WTA for this purpose. Competition among k sampled fields is coarse, however. Each update conveys at most log k nats about which mode a field should take, so the information the noise carries grows only with log k while the cost of a training step grows with k (Section 5.2.1). We therefore combine competition with distillation. Instead of constructing the coupling from the target, we distill it from the model’s own multi-step decoding under a fixed noise field. CONDOR (Co upled-Noise Distillation for One-Step Readout) has two stages. Stage 1 trains a noise-conditioned masked LM with random mask ratios and the WTA objective; this model, the teacher, can fill partially masked blocks and its fills depend on the noise. Stage 2 trains a student, initialized from the teacher, to reproduce in one forward pass the block that the teacher decodes in several steps under the same noise field; a noise-free masked-LM term on the ground truth anchors the student’s conditionals. The teacher never receives gradients; it is either the frozen Stage-1 model or an exponential moving average (EMA) of the student. At inference only the student runs, once per block. Our contributions are as follows. • We show, on a controlled TinyStories testbed with human evaluation, that competition among k sampled noise fields (WTA, and its soft form IWAE) gives the one-step output only a coarse assignment of noise to modes. The information the noise carries about the block grows with log k and stays far below the block’s entropy; one-step legality improves only slowly with k while the cost of a training step grows with k; and deeper models, larger batches, and longer training do not change this. A per-position information analysis and an information-theoretic argument account for the bound, and a sweep over k confirms it (Section 5.2.1). • We propose C ONDOR , which obtains the coupling between noise and text by distilling the network’s own multi-step decoding under a shared noise field into its one-step readout, together with keep-and-fill target construction, a noise-free ground-truth anchor, and two ways of running the teacher (frozen or EMA-updated) (Section 4). • C ONDOR raises human-evaluated one-step legality from below 10 % to 65 –73 % at one forward pass per block while different noise fields still yield different blocks; ablations identify the necessary components and characterize the failure modes of an EMA-updated teacher (Section 5.2). All experiments are conducted at small scale; our claims concern the mechanism rather than large-scale performance.

Continuous-latent diffusion language models. Diffusion-LM, DiffuSeq, and latent diffusion for language generate text by denoising in an embedding or latent space (13, 32, 37); ELF denoises in a frozen encoder’s embedding space with flow matching, Cola transports a sentence-level latent with a block-causal transformer, 3and DSL-LLaDA and CCDD retrofit a continuous noisy state onto a masked backbone (16, 21, 64, 66). In these models the sample identity lives in the initial noise, positions co-evolve before discretization, and a block costs n denoising steps. One-step generation in continuous diffusion is obtained by distillation or consistency training (11, 34, 48, 53, 63); our method is the analogue of this step for masked models, with the sequence of committed tokens in place of the continuous trajectory. 

Masked dLLMs: parallel commitment and correction. Masked diffusion language models (1–3, 36, 40, 41, 46, 51, 62) and masked generative transformers for images (7) commit several positions per step by independent argmax; ParallelBench (25) quantifies the quality loss as parallelism increases. Confidence scheduling decides which positions to commit in a step (56); editing and remasking methods revise committed tokens in later steps (3, 12, 20, 22, 26, 50, 55, 60, 61, 65), and CoCommit re-applies the final layers to a marked commit set (59). These procedures trade additional steps for consistency. Few-step distillation of discrete diffusion halves the number of steps by matching marginals (8), enables consistency distillation for uniform-state diffusion (47), distills a teacher’s correlations into a mixing variable (19), or reaches one step for masked image generators by injecting randomness through the token initialization (69). None of these gives a masked language model a noise variable that selects the block in one pass. 

Making the noise carry a mode; objectives and distillation. Latent-variable non-autoregressive trans-lation (24, 38, 52) and VADD (57) supply a latent that carries the joint of a parallel commitment and learn its coupling to the data with a VAE-style encoder or posterior that observes the target; PTP (9) constructs the coupling analytically from an AR sampler; copula and energy rescoring methods borrow joint structure from a second model at inference time (33, 58). Without an encoder, a data-independent noise input is ignored under likelihood training, as observed for conditional GANs (23, 68) and VAE posteriors (4); importance weighting over several noise samples (5) and winner-take-all (multiple-choice) losses (18, 30, 39, 45) are the standard remedies, the latter applied to noise-conditioned generators by Social GAN (17) and to language models by LoRA-MCL (35). A third way to obtain the coupling is distillation. Sequence-level knowledge distillation from an AR teacher (15, 27) makes non-autoregressive translation tractable by removing modes, since the distilled target is a deterministic function of the source (14, 43, 67). C ONDOR uses WTA only to make the teacher noise-sensitive and then distills, from the same network run for more steps, a target that is a deterministic function of the source and the noise; modes are thus indexed rather than removed. 

# 3 Preliminaries 

Masked diffusion language models. Let c denote a visible prefix and x = ( x1, . . . , x B ) a block of B

tokens to be generated. A masked dLLM pθ is a bidirectional network that, given c and a partially masked block z, outputs at every masked position i a distribution pθ (xi | c, z ) over the vocabulary. The masked positions are predicted independently given (c, z ), so each of these distributions is a marginal : the model has no direct access to the joint distribution of several masked positions. Multi-step decoding starts from the fully masked block [M] B and, at each step, commits a subset of masked positions to their predicted tokens; the committed tokens become context for subsequent steps, and the joint structure of the block is built up across steps. One-step commitment takes the argmax of the marginal pθ (· | c, [M] B ) at all B positions simultaneously.

# 4 Method 

4.1 Overview 

CONDOR trains a one-step block generator in two stages (Figure 2). Stage 1 trains a noise-conditioned masked LM, the teacher gϕ, with random mask ratios and the WTA objective. Stage 2 trains a student fθ, initialized from the teacher, to reproduce in one forward pass the block that the teacher decodes in several steps under the same noise field; a noise-free masked-LM term on the ground truth anchors the student’s conditionals. The teacher never receives gradients; it is either the frozen Stage-1 model or an exponential moving average of the student (Section 4.4). At inference only the student is used, one forward pass per block (Figure 1d).

# 5 Experiments 

5.1 Experimental setup 

Task and data. We use TinyStoriesV2-GPT4 (10) ( 2.72 M stories) tokenized with GPT-2 BPE (44) and split into non-overlapping 16-token windows. The first 8 tokens of a window form the prefix c and the remaining 

B=8 tokens the block x; we call this the 8+8 task and write 4+4 and 8+16 for the analogous tasks with a 

4-token prefix and block, and an 8-token prefix with a 16 -token block. Unless stated otherwise, the block is fully masked during training, matching the evaluation. The first 256 windows are held out from training and serve as the test set: their prefixes are the test prompts , and their blocks the reference continuations .

Models. The denoiser is a 6-layer bidirectional Transformer ( d=256 , 8 heads, FFN 1024 , learned positional embeddings, tied embeddings; 17 .6M parameters). The strongest baseline configuration uses 12 layers; 24 

and 48 layers are reported in Appendix C. In tables, a configuration such as “L12, b512, 3·10 −4, 80k” denotes 

12 layers, batch size 512 , peak learning rate 3·10 −4, and 80 k training steps. We use σ=0 .5 and k=4 unless stated otherwise. 

Baselines. The baselines are noise-conditioned one-step models (Section 4.2) trained directly on the fully masked block with one of the three Stage-1 objectives of Eq. (3); in tables they are named plain (average over 8the k fields), IWAE (log-mean-exp), and WTA (minimum), and the suffix +div indicates that the hidden-state repulsion term of Section 4.3 is added. These objectives are swept over depth, batch size, learning rate, training length, the way the block is masked during training (fully, a random subset, or a random ratio), and the layer at which ε is added (input embeddings or an intermediate layer) (Appendix C). We further sweep k for the WTA objective, and report multi-step decoding of the teacher as an upper bound on what the same weights can produce with more forward passes. Throughout, NFE (number of function evaluations) denotes the number of forward passes spent on one block. 

Evaluation protocol. For a test prompt, the model receives the prompt followed by 8 mask tokens and one noise field ε, and commits all 8 positions in a single forward pass. Only this one block, the one directly following the prompt, is generated and evaluated; the model is never asked to continue past it in the 8+8 

task. To measure the effect of the noise, the same block is generated several times for the same prompt, each time with a new, independently drawn noise field. “ 16 noise fields per prompt” therefore means 16 

alternative one-step outputs for the same 8 positions, not 16 consecutive blocks; the outputs for one prompt differ only in ε. Legality is assessed by human evaluation. A block is legal if it could appear verbatim in natural English continuing the prompt (mid-sentence cut-offs are acceptable; repeated words, punctuation runs, non-words, and semantic inconsistencies are not). Human evaluation uses the first 32 test prompts with 16 

noise fields each, i.e. 32 × 16 = 512 one-step blocks per model, each labeled legal or illegal; the protocol and its calibration are in Appendix B. Human evaluation is available for eight models; the remaining rows report an automatic proxy, marked with †, in which a pretrained GPT-2-large (44) scores each generated token by its negative log-likelihood (NLL) given the prompt and the preceding block tokens. The loose proxy calls a block legal if its mean NLL per token is below the 90th percentile of the mean NLL of the reference continuations; the strict proxy requires the block’s worst (highest-NLL) token to be below the 90th percentile of the reference continuations’ worst tokens and no two adjacent tokens to be identical. Unless stated otherwise, proxy legality means the strict proxy. On the eight human-evaluated models the strict proxy ranks the models as the human evaluation does (Spearman ρ = 0 .95 ) but inflates low legality by a factor of 2–3; proxy numbers are therefore used only for ordering variants within a table. 

Metrics. Legality (Leg.) is the fraction of the 512 one-step blocks judged legal. Legal-unique (LU) measures whether the noise field changes the output: for each prompt, among the blocks (one per noise field) judged legal, the fraction that are distinct strings, averaged over prompts. Any-of-16 is the fraction of prompts for which at least one of the 16 noise fields yields a legal block. Distinct is the number of distinct strings among the 16 blocks of a prompt (one per noise field), averaged over prompts. Repetition (Rep.) is the fraction of generated tokens equal to the token at the preceding position. Gold hit is the fraction of generated tokens equal to the reference continuation’s token at the same position; a model that predicts the most likely token at every position independently scores high on it even when the block as a whole is incoherent, so it measures closeness to the data marginals rather than quality. 5.2.1 Analysis of the noise-conditioned baselines Legality across objectives and scale. Table 1 reports one-step legality of the noise-conditioned baselines. Why competition cannot close the gap. Consider the one-step map ε 7 → ˆy(c, ε ) as a partition of the noise space into cells, each storing one continuation of c. A WTA update on a ground-truth block y samples k cells and moves the one with the lowest cross-entropy toward y. The choice is one of k alternatives, so a single update conveys at most log k nats of assignment information, independent of the continuity of ε. For the map to store N distinct continuations of c stably, the update should reach the cell assigned to y; with k independently drawn noise fields this occurs with probability 1 − (1 − 1/N )k, which equals 0.94 for N =2 , 0.27 for N =13 ,

0.04 for N =100 , and vanishes for the e16 –e20 plausible continuations of an 8-token block. Otherwise the update moves a cell that stores a different continuation toward y, and a cell updated by many different targets converges to their average, the marginal distribution. This argument makes two predictions. (i) The number of continuations a k-sample WTA map maintains scales with k rather than with the data, so the information carried by ε should grow with log k but remain far below the block entropy. (ii) The degradation concentrates at the tail of the block. The winner is selected by the block-level cross-entropy, which is dominated by the head positions on which the k candidate blocks differ, and the tail of the winning cell is subsequently overwritten by every target that shares its head. The IWAE reduction is a soft version of the same update and inherits the bound. The gap row of Table 2 is consistent with (ii); Table 3 tests (i).

# 6 Conclusion 

We asked whether a masked diffusion language model can commit a whole block of tokens in a single forward pass. A data-independent noise field is a direct way to tell the model which joint mode to produce, but none of the standard training objectives makes the field carry that information. Plain cross-entropy ignores it; importance weighting reduces to plain cross-entropy until specialization begins; and winner-take-all competition among k sampled fields conveys at most log k nats of mode assignment per example and spends it on the head of the block. C ONDOR supplies the missing training signal by distillation. A noise-conditioned teacher, trained with random mask ratios and winner-take-all, decodes a block in several steps under a fixed noise field, and a student learns to produce the same block in one pass from the same field, with a noise-free ground-truth term anchoring its conditionals. On a controlled testbed this raises human-evaluated one-step legality from below 10 % to 65 –73 % at unchanged inference cost while different noise fields still yield different blocks, and the ablations identify the shared noise field, information in the target beyond the student’s own prediction, the noise-free anchor, a teacher that models in-block conditionals and depends on the noise, and, for an EMA-updated teacher, a sufficiently slow EMA as the necessary components. Whether the same mechanism scales to longer blocks and larger models remains to be tested.

# B Human evaluation protocol and proxy calibration 

Human evaluation protocol. For each of the first 32 evaluation prompts (Section 5.1) we generate the block directly following the prompt 16 times, each time in one forward pass under an independently sampled noise field ( 16 alternative blocks for the same 8 positions, not a continuation), and label each of the 32 × 16 = 512 

blocks given its prompt. A block is legal if the 8-token window could appear verbatim in natural English continuing the prompt. Mid-sentence cut-offs are legal, since any 8-token window of real text is a fragment. A block is illegal if it contains adjacent word repetition ( was was ), punctuation or ellipsis runs, non-words or broken token boundaries, or a semantic inconsistency ( boy named Sarah. The loose proxy rewards repetition, since a language model assigns high probability to a repeated token: the collapsed whale ×8 output passes 78 .5% of the time, and the k-sweep appears to double (18 → 30 %) where human evaluation gives 5.3 → 6.4%. # D k-sweep details 

The sweep re-runs the 8+8 short configuration (L6, b256, 3·10 −4, cosine, 100 k steps, σ=0 .5) changing only 

k. For k ≥ 64 the logits of k × 256 rows over a 50 k vocabulary do not fit in memory; since only the winner receives gradient under pure WTA, the winner is selected in chunks without gradient and only the winning row is recomputed with gradient. The two paths agree to 10 −6 in loss and select identical winners on a k=8 test batch. The re-run at k=4 reproduces the original arm ( 17 .8† vs. 16 .4† loose). Per-position, raising k from 4

to 16 doubles the peak loser-minus-winner gap ( 1.50 → 3.00 nats at position 3) while the tail gap rises only to 1.1: the winner is decided even more firmly by the head. Winner cross-entropy falls 4.21 → 3.78 → 3.36 

(k=4 , 16 , 64 ). 

# E Synthetic two-mode study: WTA suffices when N ≤ k

The lookup-table argument of Section 5.2.1 predicts that WTA does make the ticket work when the number of modes per context is at most of order k. A synthetic task checks this and isolates Proposition 1 and Remark 1. 

Construction. 512 contexts (4-token prefixes over a 64-token vocabulary), each with exactly two planted completions A, B of length L that differ at every position. Training samples a context, one completion and a random non-empty mask subset; a 0.81 M-parameter bidirectional transformer ( 4 layers, d=128 ) reconstructs the masked tokens with σ=0 .5, k=4 , batch 256 , AdamW 3·10 −4, cosine. Memorization is expected in this synthetic control; the question is whether the positions of a one-step generation coordinate to produce either planted completion rather than mixing them. Evaluation is one step, fully masked, with 1000 independently sampled noise fields per context: legality is exact equality with A or B (2 · 2−L for a per-position coin flip); a context is balanced if its legal outputs are 30 –70 % A and polarized if < 10 % or > 90 %. 

Findings. Plain cross-entropy climbs to 0.45 legality within 10 k steps and then descends to the coin-flip floor as optimization drives every position to its marginal, consistent with Proposition 1. WTA at L=4 /8

reaches 0.89 /0.81 with every context balanced and none polarized: with N =2 modes and k=4 noise fields per update, the probability that a WTA step lands in the right cell is 94 % and the table is stable, as Section 5.2.1 predicts. The transition is late (at L=8 : 0.14 at 20 k, 0.75 at 40 k). IWAE tracks plain CE into collapse at 

L=4 and overtakes WTA at L=16 (0.74 vs. 0.62 ), where the sequence-sum softmax has hardened (Remark 1).

# G Implementation details 

Data stream. We scan the released TinyStoriesV2-GPT4 JSONL in file order. Each story is tokenized separately and split from its beginning into non-overlapping complete windows; incomplete tails are discarded in the 8+8 experiments. The first 256 emitted windows form the evaluation set, and training starts at window 

257 . When the file is exhausted, the stream rewinds. The released per-block label files identify the first 32 

evaluation windows used for human judgment. 

Stage-1 models. Bidirectional Transformer, pre-norm, learned positions over the window, tied embeddings, GPT-2 vocabulary plus [M] (plus a padding token in block mode). k=4 noise fields per example in one batched forward. Short 8+8 configuration: L6, batch 256 , AdamW 3·10 −4, cosine, 100 k steps. Long: L6, batch 512 , 4·10 −4, warm-up–stable–decay schedule, 500 k steps. Best grid cell: L12, batch 1024 , 10 −3, cosine, 

80 k. The Stage-1 teacher (run name ts9rm-wta in the released code) is the long configuration with a uniformly random number of masked block positions per example and WTA without repulsion. For baseline 26 rows carrying the suffix +div , the repulsion weight is λdiv = 0 .1 (not annealed), with Ldiv as described in Section 4.3. 

Distillation. Student initialized from the teacher; 50 k steps, batch 512 , AdamW 10 −4, cosine, 500 warm-up steps; σ=0 .5; the teacher fills re-masked positions one token per step, most confident position first, by argmax, under the same field with σT =0 .5; keep-and-fill ρ=0 .5, T =4 ; full decoding ( ρ=0 ) T =8 ; anchor λ=1

β ∈ { 0.99 , 0.999 , 0.9999 }. Conceptually, the noisy branch uses the first student distributions directly in 

Ldistill (Algorithm 1). The released implementation instead computes those distributions without gradients while constructing the label, then recomputes the same logits with gradients before the loss. This avoids retaining the student activation graph across the teacher’s multi-step fill. Because the denoiser uses zero dropout and the weights are unchanged between the two calls, the logits and objective are identical. The noise-free anchor has a different input and always requires a separate student call. The runs that skip Stage 1 (random initialization, EMA-updated teacher) use 150 k steps at 3·10 −4 with the distillation weight ramped from 0 at 50 k to 1 at 100 k steps. Checkpoint resumption re-seeds with (seed + step) and fast-forwards the data stream so that no (window, ε) pair is replayed; the evaluation noise generator and the Gumbel projection
